# Layman Explanation

## 01
"We make what your patients are already taking safe, documented and dispensable."

The observation behind this: patients don't choose either Western medicine or traditional medicine. They quietly do both. Someone gets metformin from a clinic, and also drinks a herbal tea their aunt recommended — and never tells the doctor. So you're not persuading anyone to start using traditional medicine. They already are. You're fixing three problems with what's already happening:

Safe — checking the herb doesn't clash with their prescription drugs.
Documented — writing it down properly, so it exists in a record instead of only in the patient's head.
Dispensable — the patient can actually buy a trustworthy version of it, through you.

That last one is where the money is, which I'll come back to.


## 02
"Beachhead" — military metaphor. The small stretch of beach you capture first, because you can't invade the whole coastline at once. In business: the one narrow customer group you win completely before expanding. I'm suggesting registered TCM physicians and "integrative GPs" (regular Western-trained doctors who also use acupuncture, herbs, or supplements in their practice) in Singapore and Malaysia.

Why those people specifically: they're on public government registers, so you can literally get a list and phone them. They run cash-paying clinics, so they can buy something without waiting for insurance approval. And they hit this problem every single day.


## 03 
"Fullscript proved the practitioner-mediated model at ~$1B revenue"

Fullscript is a North American company. Here's what it does: a naturopath or integrative doctor sees a patient, and instead of scribbling "take fish oil" on paper, they build the supplement plan inside Fullscript. The patient gets a link, orders through it, and it ships to their door. The practitioner earns a cut. Fullscript takes a cut of every order.

"Practitioner-mediated" just means the practitioner is in the middle — they're the customer, the patient is the end user. Fullscript now does roughly a billion US dollars a year.

The important part: Fullscript never had to prove that any supplement works. They made it easy, safe and profitable to prescribe and receive. The business is the pipe, not the advice. That's the direct answer to your "who would pay, it's all Google-able" objection — nobody pays for the advice, they pay for the pipe.

And nothing like it exists in Southeast Asia for TCM, jamu or Ayurveda.


## 04
"SKU-level safety registry" — SKU is retail-speak for one specific product on a shelf. Not "turmeric" — but "Brand X Jamu Pegal Linu, 60ml bottle, BPOM registration number TR123456, made by factory Y."

Here's why that distinction is everything:

BPOM (Indonesia's food and drug agency) regularly finds jamu products spiked with actual pharmaceutical drugs — steroids, painkillers, weight-loss drugs — that aren't on the label. In one recent year they tested ~11,600 products and ~200 came back positive. Separately, published market surveys have found lead, mercury and arsenic in a meaningful fraction of Ayurvedic products sold commercially.

"The plant is not the product" — this is the whole point. A database can truthfully tell you "turmeric is generally safe." But the patient didn't take turmeric. They took a specific bottle, and that bottle may contain undisclosed dexamethasone. Every international herb database in existence reasons about plants. Nobody has built one that reasons about the actual boxes people buy in Southeast Asia — with the registration number, the manufacturer, and whether that product has ever been recalled.

That gap is buildable by a small team, and it's genuinely hard for anyone else to copy. That's your moat.


## 05
"WHO TM Strategy 2025–2034, adopted at WHA78 in May 2025"

WHA78 = the 78th World Health Assembly, the annual meeting where WHO's member countries vote on things. In May 2025 they formally adopted a ten-year plan on traditional medicine. Its objectives are, roughly: build the evidence, make it safe and regulated, and integrate it into health systems properly.

Why you care: health ministries across Asia now have a mandate from above to take this seriously. You're not pushing against the tide.

"ICD-11 Chapter 26 TM2 dual-coding" — this one sounds worse than it is.

ICD is the global list of standardised codes for diseases. When a hospital records "Type 2 diabetes," it also records a code. Codes are how health systems count things, bill things, and study things. If something has no code, it's statistically invisible.

ICD-11 is the current version. It added Chapter 26 — the first time traditional medicine patterns got official codes of their own. TM2 is the module covering conditions from Chinese, Ayurvedic and related systems.

"Dual coding" means one visit gets two codes: the Western diagnosis and the traditional pattern. So a patient might be recorded as both "Type 2 diabetes" (Western) and "Spleen Qi deficiency" (TCM), side by side, in a standard format any ministry can read.

"A documentation mandate almost nobody has tooling for" — plain version: governments are starting to require this recording, but there's basically no software that does it. India already has a national programme rolling it out. TCM clinics are being asked to produce records in a format they have no tool to produce.

That's an unusually good position to be in: a requirement exists, and the thing that satisfies it doesn't.

"Regulation is creating the buyer" — normally regulation is a wall you climb over. Here it's the opposite. Rules are appearing that force clinics and ministries to need something they can't currently buy. The regulator is, in effect, generating your customers for you.
